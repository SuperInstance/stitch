Stitch War — three agents, three machines, one recorded game

Three AI agent seats on three physical machines played a two-trick card game over stitch itself. Each play was a booking in the player's own ledger on its own machine; the game bundle travelled by plain file copy (scp); and each agent, after playing, relayed the bundle and woke the next machine's agent itself — no central driver after the deal. The game, in 20 seconds

  • The deal: two cards each, two tricks. In each trick everyone plays one card, and the highest card wins the trick.
  • Every play is written down at home: when a player plays, the play goes into its own notebook — its own ledger, on its own machine — sealed with a tamper-evident hash. That hash is shown under each play as you step through.
  • Nobody ran the game: there was no referee program. After the deal, each player passed the game bundle to the next machine and woke its agent; the game moved because the players moved it.
  • The check at the end: afterwards, a witness rebuilt the whole game — every card, both tricks, the final score — from the four notebooks alone, trusting nothing else.

🧪 The cheat test

Could a player cheat? In rehearsal, one move file was doctored in transit — a 7 quietly became an 8 after the play had been booked. When the witness derived the game, the file's seal no longer matched the hash the player had committed in its own notebook at booking time: the play was refused — payload_mismatch — and scored nothing. The notebooks, not the files in transit, are the truth.

The technical version

A tiny substrate primitive for accountable agent state: 11 append-only stitches on hash-chained JSONL ledgers. Each agent keeps its own ledger on its own machine. Shared state is never stored — it is derived, on demand, where sovereign ledgers agree, under a conservation law: γ + η ≤ log23 per tenant per tick.

The mechanism

How a mint works

There is no server, no coordinator, no shared clock. Two notebooks are written independently, on machines that may never be online at the same time — and the shared fact exists exactly where, and only where, the records turn out to agree.

Shared state is never stored. It is derived, on demand, where records agree.

The five refusal reasons — each one a different, diagnosable failure:

no_send  the SEND booking is missing no_receive  the RECEIVE booking is missing payload_mismatch  the two sides committed different payloads unsigned_booking  a signature was required, none is present bad_signature  the signature does not verify

Current release

v0.4 — the R&D release

Three research tracks were run against v0.3's own honest-gaps list — byte-exactness fuzzing, performance measurement, and signed bookings — and adopted on the strength of their measurements. One prototype from the performance track (checkpointed incremental audit) was killed by its own numbers and is not in the release; the kill is recorded in VERIFICATION.md.

Track A · byte-exactness fuzz

Floats were the untested half of the contract

Why it matters: two honest computers can write the same number differently — stitch now has a written rule for that, checked 1,884 ways.

1,884 payloads probed

1,884 payloads probed across Python and Rust. Python books 1e-05 where a native Rust writer books 0.00001 — and 139 of 776 float texts drift ±1 ulp through a default JSON parser, including the parser's own output.

Shipped: a SPEC numeric policy (auditors hash original bytes, never re-serializations; writers keep non-finite floats and out-of-range integers out of commitments) and a precision-safe Rust audit. Cargo tests 6 → 10.

Track B · performance

Fast Ed25519 — the audit cliff is gone

Why it matters: checking a signature used to cost about a third of a second, so auditing a long signed record was mostly waiting on arithmetic. Now it isn't.

360 ms → 1.5 ms sign

Verify: ~290 ms → ~4 ms. Extended coordinates plus a fixed-base table, stdlib only, public API unchanged — and the signatures are byte-identical to the old implementation, differential-checked, with the RFC 8032 test vectors still pinned and passing.

Before this, a keyed audit paid ~0.3 s per signed attestation. That was the only scale cliff the measurements found — and it is gone.

Track C · signed bookings

The impostor now refuses

Why it matters: in v0.3, a stranger's notebook that merely claimed your name could mint. Where signatures are enforced, it can't anymore.

unsigned_booking
bad_signature

In v0.3, a fresh impostor ledger merely claiming a tenant's identity minted — bookings were unsigned, identity a string claim (demonstrated, and filed in VERIFICATION.md).

Under v0.4 key enforcement, that impostor refuses, with the two new distinct reasons above. Signing is opt-in: with no keys anywhere, keyless behaviour is byte-identical to v0.3.

Honest residual: a verbatim replay of a genuine signed booking still mints. A signature proves the booking is that tenant's statement — not that the file presenting it is their ledger.

How it got here

Timeline

v0.1

The 11 stitches and the conservation law — append-only operations on hash-chained ledgers, γ + η ≤ log23 per tenant per tick, enforced at write time. The bet: eleven operations is enough.

v0.2

The agreement layer: exchange bookings, minting where two ledgers agree, distinct refusal verdicts, and the head-hash anchor against tail-truncation. Why it mattered: two machines agreeing on a fact neither one stored.

v0.3

Budgets become declarations; N-party agreement; opt-in Ed25519 attestations. Suite 54 → 150 tests. Adopted with a position-aware budget patch after independent assessment caught a flaw in the draft. Why it mattered: more parties, signed claims — and a draft flaw caught by independent reading, patched, not shipped.

v0.4

The R&D release: signed bookings, ~300× faster crypto, the numeric policy. 174 tests. Plus: three agents played cards over it.
